Simulation is where the IOC stops reacting and starts rehearsing. It takes the live state of the destination and carries it forward under a chosen pressure, a surge of arrivals, a fleet drawn down, a wind shift onto the reef, and shows where the day would strain before it actually does. The point is not to predict the future precisely; it is to surface the squeeze early enough to act on it.

Every run is built on the same spine, whatever the persona: a trigger that puts load on the island, the forecasted effect of that load on capacity, and a response that closes the gap. Read that way, a simulation is never just a number on a panel. It is a short causal story: this is what we are putting on the island, this is what it does to the system, and this is what we would do about it.

That spine is why the layer earns its place. A run surfaces a problem while the schedule, the fleet, the routing, or the day's marine plan can all still be changed, rather than after a guest is already waiting at the Marina or a reef is already over its dive capacity. The shift is from explaining a problem after it has been felt to seeing it build and moving first.

## How a run works

Every run is an act of selection, not authoring, and it follows one of two rhythms depending on the simulation.

The operational runs, Crowd and Traffic, choose an event to simulate, set the parameters that size it, choose an action plan to test against it, run it, read the forecast, then implement the plan that holds up best. The event and the action plan are both drawn from a prepared library, so the run is selection and comparison rather than authoring, and the chosen plan is dispatched to the teams.

The condition runs, the environment and marine simulations, choose a scenario preset, set the boxed inputs that size it, run it, read the forecast, then weigh the recommended responses. These are exploration runs: they dispatch nothing, and the lowest response, Do nothing, either holds at the modeled level or, for energy, trends to the projected worst.

Reading any run means reading that chain back out of the screen. The left of the page is the cause: what is being put on the island and how hard. The center is the system under that load: the twin showing where the pressure lands. The right is the consequence and the choices: the forecasted outcome, the metrics that move, and the responses that would bend the curve.

## The page at a glance

The layout is the same across every simulation, so an operator learns it once and then only the controls and the metrics change. Three columns do the work:

- Left, the setup: the scenario preset or event, the inputs that scale it (volume, presets, boxed variables), the layers shown, and where it applies, the action plan to test. Run Simulation commits the scenario and starts the forecast.
- Center, the digital twin: the Shura Island twin carrying the visualization layer. The run is drawn straight onto the island as a density heatmap, a traffic heatmap, a wind field, or a coastal overlay, so a developing problem reads as a place an operator recognizes rather than a figure they have to interpret.
- Right, the forecast: the forecasted outcome banner, the forecasted conditions and KPIs that move under the scenario, and the recommended responses.

The operational runs move through three states, from setup to run to implement. The condition runs share the first two and stop there, since they explore rather than dispatch.

**Default, setup.** The operator composes the scenario on the left while the twin shows the island live and the right reads current conditions. Nothing is forecasted yet. This state is the baseline the run will be measured against, so it is worth a glance before committing.

<SimState state="running" />

**Running, the forecast.** Running the scenario carries it forward in time. The twin lights up with the forecasted pattern, where crowd density gathers, where vehicle traffic thickens, or where wind and water cross a limit, and the right panel resolves into the forecasted outcome, the metrics and KPIs that shift, and the recommended responses. This is the moment the squeeze becomes visible while it is still a forecast.

<SimState state="implement" />

**Implementation, acting.** On the operational runs, choosing an action re-runs the forecast with that action applied, so the outcome and the metrics shift to show its effect, and the chosen action is marked while the rest stay open to compare. Implement routes the dispatch to the relevant teams, moves the action into the live operations view, and re-anchors the forecast baseline so the real outcome can be tracked against what was forecast. The condition runs do not reach this state: their responses are modeled previews, not dispatched actions.

## Where the models come from

The IOC is the cockpit, not the modeling engine. It does not author events or forecasts, because the modeling behind them is heavy, calibrated work that belongs in specialist tools. The operational runs draw on pedestrian and traffic models: crowd events are modeled in MassMotion, and vehicle and traffic events in PTV and Aimsun. Those tools publish into the IOC as the library an operator selects from.

<SimSources />

**Reading it.** Everything left of the divider is authored upstream and handed to the IOC as a ready library. Everything right of it happens in the twin: the operator picks an event and an action plan, sets the parameters, runs it, and the twin visualizes the scenario, then surfaces the forecasted outcome and the recommended actions on the island. The split keeps the operator on the decision, not on configuring a model. The twin runs and shows the scenario; it does not build it.

The condition runs are published the same way, each from its own engine: HOMER Pro for energy, Ladybug Tools for outdoor thermal comfort, WRF plus CFD for wind, Delft3D for flood and storm surge, Delft3D-WAQ for water quality, Delft3D-FLOW for ocean current, SWAN for wave, and ReefMod-GBR for coral reef. The operator sets a scenario rather than picking a prepared event, but the division holds: the engine models, the twin shows.

## The simulations

The simulations share the spine and the page, and differ in the persona they serve, the place they are scoped to, the pressure they model, and whether they dispatch or explore. They fall into two families.

The operational what-ifs select an event and an action plan and can dispatch the response:

- Crowd, the guest experience persona, scoped to the Marina. The trigger is an arrival surge; the effect is guests building up faster than the fleet can clear them; the response is the mobility plan that keeps them moving. The run is read against the mobility health index, with vehicles and chargers as the binding constraints.
- Traffic, the operations persona, scoped to the road network and the fleet. The trigger is peak vehicle and charging load; the effect is utilization, charging, and road segments approaching capacity; the response is rebalancing, diverting, and routing assets so the network holds. The run is read against fleet utilization and the capacity outlook.

The condition forecasts set a scenario and read it against a threshold, as exploration rather than dispatch:

- Energy and Outdoor thermal comfort, the environment persona, scoped to Shura Island. The trigger is a demand and supply setting or a heat setting; the effect is renewable share falling toward diesel backup, or shade running short at peak sun; the response is dispatch and reserve choices, or canopy and shading.
- Wind, Flood and storm surge, Water quality, Ocean current, Wave, and Coral reef, the marine persona, scoped to North Reef. The trigger is a wind, surge, water, current, wave, or heat-stress setting; the effect is a marine or environmental threshold being approached, the small-craft window, the vessel-risk level, dive clarity, drift, or the reef closure trigger; the response is holding, rerouting, restricting, or resting to protect both guests and the coast.
