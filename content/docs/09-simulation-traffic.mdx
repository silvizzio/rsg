The Traffic domain covers movement and the systems that carry it: guest mobility, the shuttle network, the drop-off forecourt, the island microgrid that powers all of it, and, for Marine Operations, the water-flow conditions that govern how vessels move. Each persona below runs the sims relevant to its remit, read through its own lens.

The scope label in the tool reads Shura Island as a placeholder and is not load-bearing in these descriptions.

## Guest Experience Manager

### Mobility

Guest mobility is the connective tissue of the destination, and it tends to fail quietly: demand builds at a station, the wait for a ride creeps up, and the first real signal is a guest who has already been standing too long. The gap between transfers running to plan and transfers running behind is where the experience is won or lost.

This run takes a demand scenario and plays it forward against the fleet and the schedule, surfacing where transfer times stretch and requests pile up before anyone is left waiting. It gives the team the lead time to anticipate the pinch, so the response is a measured adjustment ahead of the surge rather than a recovery after the queue has already formed.

![Mobility, Guest Experience read.](03-gxm-lod3-simulation-traffic-mobility.jpg)

### Shuttle network

A shuttle network is only as good as its weakest link, and guests feel that link as a route they have learned not to trust. When one leg runs unreliably, the whole network quietly loses people to walking, which reshapes demand everywhere else in ways that are hard to see from a single timetable.

This run plays the network forward to show where reliance concentrates and where guests fall back to walking, exposing the legs that carry the most weight and the ones that are being quietly abandoned. It lets the team reason about the network as a whole, rather than tuning one route at a time and discovering the knock-on effects later.

![Shuttle network, Guest Experience read.](03-gxm-lod3-simulation-traffic-shuttle-network.jpg)

### Drop-off forecourt

The forecourt is the first and last impression of an arrival, and it is also where small delays compound fastest. A few vehicles dwelling too long at the kerb turns a smooth drop-off into a backed-up approach, and the queue forms in exactly the place a guest is meant to feel welcomed.

This run plays an arrival pattern forward through the forecourt, reading how long vehicles dwell, how the queue builds, and how the bays fill. It surfaces the point where the forecourt tips from flowing to congested, so staging and kerb management can be set ahead of the rush rather than improvised once the approach is already blocked.

![Drop-off forecourt, Guest Experience read.](03-gxm-lod3-simulation-traffic-drop-off.jpg)

## Operations Manager

### Mobility

From the operations side, mobility is a question of whether the assets can meet the day. The same demand the guest experiences as a wait lands on operations as a capacity calculation: how many vehicles are in service, how the charging keeps up, and whether the whole system has any slack left when the peak hits.

This run plays the day's load forward against the fleet and the charging network, surfacing where the system runs flat-out and where it tips over. It shows the binding constraint, whether it is vehicles, charging, or both, so the team can rebalance before the peak rather than discover the shortfall in the middle of it.

![Mobility, Operations read.](placeholder-ops-mobility.jpg)

### Energy

The island microgrid sits underneath everything that moves, and its failure mode is gradual rather than sudden: renewable supply slips, the battery buffer thins, and at some point the system leans on diesel backup. By the time that happens the window to avoid it has closed.

This run plays a demand and supply scenario forward against the renewable balance, showing when the share of clean supply drifts toward the point where backup is called and how much reserve is left behind it. It hands the team the lead time to shape dispatch, charging, and reserve while there is still room to act, keeping a supply gap from compounding into a diesel call.

![Energy, Operations read.](placeholder-ops-energy.jpg)

### Shuttle network

Read from the operations side, the shuttle network is a fleet-and-route problem rather than a guest-trust one. The concern is how the vehicles and the routes hold up under load, where utilization concentrates, and which legs are carrying more than they comfortably can.

This run plays the network forward from the asset's perspective, surfacing the routes that run hot and the capacity that is being stretched. It lets the team see where to add or rebalance service before a leg saturates, keeping the network's reliability from quietly eroding under a load it was never sized for.

![Shuttle network, Operations read.](placeholder-ops-shuttle-network.jpg)

### Drop-off forecourt

For operations, the forecourt is a throughput and staging problem. The question is whether the kerb, the bays, and the staff working them can clear vehicles fast enough to keep the approach moving, and where that clearance breaks down under an arrival surge.

This run plays the arrival pattern forward against the forecourt's capacity, showing where staging falls behind and the queue starts to back into the approach. It gives the team the basis to position staff and manage the kerb ahead of the rush, rather than reacting once the forecourt is already the bottleneck.

## Environment Manager

### Energy

For the environment team, the microgrid is read as a sustainability question rather than a supply one. The concern is how much of the load is met by renewables, what carbon the mix is costing, and whether usable clean generation is being wasted while the reserve runs thin.

This run plays a demand and supply scenario forward against the renewable balance and reads the climate cost of the drift: the renewable share, the emissions, the curtailment, and the reserve. It lets the team see where the clean-energy story is holding and where it is slipping, so dispatch and reserve choices can protect the renewable commitment, not just keep the lights on.

## Marine Operations Manager

### Wave

Sea state at the marina and along the routes decides whether small craft can run, and it can change faster than a schedule can react. A workable morning can build into a closing window, and the call to hold craft is far better made early than once a boat is already exposed.

This run plays an offshore swell scenario forward to the marina and the routes, showing where wave height climbs toward the limit for small craft and where waves break on the lines boats actually run. It surfaces the closing window ahead of time, so departures and beach calls can be made with margin rather than under pressure.

![Wave, Marine Operations read.](06-mo-lod3-simulation-wave.jpg)

### Ocean current

Current at the channel is the condition that quietly raises the stakes on a dive day. A flow that is manageable at slack can strengthen enough to make drift a real hazard, and because it concentrates at the channel rather than the average, it is easy to underread from a single number.

This run plays a tidal and wind scenario forward to show how current builds at the channel and along the dive routes, separating the site reading from the broad average. It gives the team the lead time to adjust dive routing and vessel calls before boats are out, rather than managing a stronger-than-expected flow once they already are.

![Ocean current, Marine Operations read.](06-mo-lod3-simulation-ocean-current.jpg)
